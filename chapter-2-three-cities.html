Chapter 02 / Three Cities, One Bike

Every city
taught me
a different lesson.

Same job, same platform, same bike. Three different Dutch cities decided completely differently how much I earned, how long I rode, and how customers treated me. This is the fingerprint of each.

City 01 / Apeldoorn
Aug – Oct 2020 · 60 days

Apeldoorn.
The starter town.

A new city on Uber's map. A student rider on a promotional program. A restaurant scene of about five kitchens, and 90% of them were McDonald's.

60%
Of trips triggered surge pricing

Uber was launching a new market. They needed riders on the road. So every second trip fired surge pricing — an incentive to keep me pedaling in a town too small to sustain the demand naturally.

City 02 / Amersfoort
Oct 2020 – Feb 2021 · 97 days

Amersfoort.
The favorite.

More restaurants had come online. The map filled in. And an entire Dutch winter — the coldest, hardest lockdown of the pandemic — happened here, on this bike.

€18.11
Median hourly rate · highest of any city

The best-paying city per active hour I ever rode. Enough variety of restaurants to keep me moving. Enough winter to bring out sympathetic tippers. Enough demand to earn €6.47 per trip — the highest per-trip average of the three.

City 03 / Utrecht
Mar – Oct 2021 · 137 days

Utrecht.
The mature market.

Every restaurant on multiple platforms. Every rider on multiple apps. The Eureka moment — when I stopped seeing individual orders and started seeing the shape of the whole system.

19.8%
Of trips were batched — highest rate anywhere

A dense city with a dense restaurant map means Uber could pair orders together. In Apeldoorn, batching was 1.4%. Here it was 20%. The system had matured to the point where two deliveries could share one bike ride.

05 / Three Fingerprints

Same job.
Different math.

Every metric on this page changed as I moved cities. The market matured. Uber pulled back on surge. Batching went up. Sessions got longer. My earnings per trip went down — but total earnings went up.

Look at the diagonals. Surge fell as the market matured. Batching rose. Session length stretched. Every column is a snapshot of Uber's platform at a different stage of its own growth — and I was riding across all three stages.

06 / What the Map Was Actually Showing

Apeldoorn taught me the promo. Amersfoort taught me the winter. Utrecht taught me the system.

The three cities weren't just three geographies. They were three stages of Uber's own maturity — a small market bribing riders to show up, a mid-sized market humming at its best hourly rate, a large market where the algorithm had learned to squeeze two deliveries into one bike ride.

I moved three times in fourteen months. But the platform was moving too, faster than I noticed, restructuring the work under me while I pedaled.

Only in the data, a year later, could I see all three of us moving at once.
