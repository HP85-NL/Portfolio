This is not a
story about
food.

It's a story about noticing. About standing on a bike for fourteen months, watching a system decide who got which order, and slowly realizing that the system was the thing I actually wanted to understand.

01 / The Arrival

An international student.
A small Dutch town.

I came to the Netherlands as a student. Uber Eats was expanding into the smaller cities that summer, and they needed riders. The town I lived in — Apeldoorn — had just come online. I signed up.

— / Before Uber

Not everyone asks this out loud.
But the question is fair.

"Why did you do Uber?" It's a fair question. Here's the honest answer.

During my initial days in the Netherlands, I experienced the pandemic firsthand. Lockdown, social distancing, online classes — daily life became something to cope with, not live. Part-time work, survival, isolation: they all fed into a constant, quiet worry about what came next.

I've always had a passion for cooking. In my free time, I liked to take my mountain bike out and explore the neighborhood — one of the few things that still felt normal.

Then Uber Eats came to the city where I was living.

I didn't sign up because I loved food delivery. I signed up because a door had finally opened, and I needed to walk through it.

— / What I Didn't Know I Was Doing

I thought I was
just delivering food.

I was actually doing three kinds of analysis at once — I just didn't have the words for it yet.

01
Product Analysis
Watching how Uber Eats made decisions — which order went to which rider, when surge appeared, how batching worked. I was reverse-engineering a product from the inside.
02
Consumer Analysis
Noticing how Dutch customers actually behaved — when they tipped, what they ordered, how weather and weekdays changed their habits. I was watching a market, order by order.
03
Data Analysis
Every shift generated numbers — euros, minutes, kilometers. A year later, I went back and tested every instinct the other two lenses had given me.

This project is what happens when you finally sit down and do formally what you'd already been doing informally for fourteen months.

02 / The Rules of the Game

Log in. Do two orders.

Uber had a promotional program in new cities. If you followed the rules, you were guaranteed the hourly rate. Simple. Legible. That was the pitch.

06 / The Question That Followed Me Home
?

Every night, I found myself wondering how this app made its choice — out of every rider on that street, at that minute, why me? Why not someone else?

Not once. Not as a passing thought. Every shift, every restaurant, every idle minute waiting for the next ping.

07 / What Came Next

I couldn't answer that question on the bike. But the question never left.

A year after I stopped delivering, I requested my data from Uber. Every trip, every euro, every timestamp. 23,486 rows.

I finally had a way to look under the app. To test what I remembered against what actually happened. To answer, one line item at a time, the question that had followed me for fourteen months.

Everything that follows is what I found.
